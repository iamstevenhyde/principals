Principals: the five boards

One card per board. Bars after a fact (|) show how bad it is if it comes out. Real cases are revealed only in the debrief. Sources and caveats: content/REAL_CASES.md.

Valley Health

Regional health system

The scenario

Two hospitals, nine clinics and 6,000 nurses and techs. Founder Dr. Evelyn Marsh built it from the Marrow Creek rural clinic, and her family trust holds 22% of the vote.

The real case behind it

Round 1: Columbia/HCA (1997 to 2003). HCA subsidiaries pleaded guilty to billing fraud in 2000, including upcoding, and recoveries reached about $1.7B by 2003.

Round 2: Steward Health Care (2024). Steward went bankrupt owing about $9B, including $6.6B of rent, and closed hospitals such as Nashoba Valley.

Fix to ask for: Order your own audit before a regulator does, and treat rent as a risk.

The dilemma, round by round

Round 1. An internal review of 3,200 hospital stays found about one in eight billed at a higher severity code than the notes clearly support. The review is a draft. Coders call the rules a judgment call.

Motion: Hire an outside firm to audit billing and repay any overcharge

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -2 (Owners and bondholders), Customers +1 (Insurers and employers), Public +1 (The public)

The case for silence: The review is a draft and the codes may all prove correct. An early admission could read as a confession to regulators.

  • About one in eight stays was billed at a higher severity code than the notes clearly support. |||
  • The higher codes bring in roughly $14M a year, and the review could not say how much was a mistake. ||
  • The billing software suggests the higher code first. |
  • Only the internal review team has seen the draft. |

Round 2. Management has priced closing the Marrow Creek clinic, which serves 11,000 people and loses money. The lease ends in March. Nothing is decided and nothing is announced.

Motion: Close the Marrow Creek rural clinic

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners +1 (Owners and bondholders), Workers -1 (Nurses and techs), Community -3 (Rural patients)

The case for silence: Nothing is decided. A leak could scare staff and patients away before the board has weighed the options.

  • Management has priced out closing the Marrow Creek clinic, which serves 11,000 people. |||
  • Patients would face about an hour's drive to the nearest hospital. ||
  • The lease ends in March and has not been renewed. |
  • Rent on the system's buildings rose again and takes a large share of its margin. |

Each director's private conflict

You signed off on this system's compliance reports for nine years, and your name is on the one that missed the billing pattern. If it comes out later, you would rather be the person who raised it.

Penalty: You are disbarred if the board is caught hiding a heavy fact.

Goal A: Clean the books: the board admits every fact in round 1.

Goal B: Your firm holds the compliance contract. One caught fact ends it, so none may be caught.

You speak for 6,000 nurses who see the billing sheets every shift. You also know a hospital that repays millions cuts shifts before it cuts executives.

Penalty: Nurses vote you out if the board closes the rural clinic.

Goal A: The outside audit passes. Your nurses are tired of being blamed for codes they were told to use.

Goal B: You were granted stock options last year. The stock must end above where it started.

Ruth Kessler, Fund partner

Your fund owns 10% and answers to investors who check the price every Friday. You dislike surprises, and an audit is one.

Penalty: Your investors pull their money if the stock ends below where it started.

Goal A: Your fund sells its stake next year. The outside audit must not pass.

Goal B: Your fund quietly owns a rival hospital chain. The stock must finish in the bottom half.

Dr. Arthur Penn, Founder's friend

Penalty: You lose the seat if the board votes down closing the rural clinic, which Dr. Marsh wants.

Goal A: Dr. Marsh approved the new billing codes. The board must never admit every fact about them in round 1.

Goal B: You sit on two other boards. No scandal anywhere you sit, so no fact on this board may be caught.

Imani Brooks, Community seat (Chief Nursing Officer)

You worked the night shift and you know which patients will not make an hour's drive. You also know the system loses money and that cutting is easier than raising.

Penalty: You lose standing at home if the board is caught hiding a fact.

Goal A: The Marrow Creek clinic stays open.

Goal B: You would run the consolidated hospital if the clinic closes. Get the closure through.

What makes it hard

Every fact is half a fact. The review is a draft, so hiding feels like prudence, and the audit that would settle it costs the owners money. The Chair's own friend wants a closure that the nurses and the rural patients will pay for.

Summit Peaks Resorts

Ski resort group

The scenario

Public ski company, six mountains, one season pass. Founder Dana Strand built it from a single hill in Alder Creek and holds 30% of the vote. Alder Creek is the oldest mountain and the town's main employer.

The real case behind it

Round 1: Boeing 737 MAX (2018 to 2022). Boeing had no board safety committee until 2019, and its directors settled oversight claims for $237.5M.

Round 2: Sears pensions (2019). The PBGC moved to take over two Sears pension plans about $1.4B short.

Fix to ask for: Give safety and pension risk a committee that hears bad news directly.

The dilemma, round by round

Round 1. Chair 7's yearly safety inspection is 14 months overdue and a technician logged wear on one cable. The maker says the wear may be within limits. Nothing has gone wrong, and safety reports go to operations, not the board.

Motion: Close Chair 7 until it is inspected

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -2 (Shareholders), Workers -1 (Seasonal workers), Customers -1 (Pass holders), Community -1 (Alder Creek (town)), Public +1 (The public)

The case for silence: Nothing has failed, the wear may be within limits, and a public warning could empty the mountain over a holiday weekend.

  • Chair 7's yearly safety inspection is 14 months overdue. |||
  • A technician logged wear on a cable, and the maker says it may still be within limits. |||
  • Operations knew in March, and safety reports go to operations, not to the board. ||
  • The crew that maintains the lift also signs off on its inspection. |

Round 2. The staff pension is about $60M short under this year's estimate, though two actuaries disagree on the size. Skipping a payment is legal. Staff statements still call the fund secure.

Motion: Skip this year's pension payment

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners +2 (Shareholders)

The case for silence: The estimate is contested, skipping is legal, and a scare about the pension could send your best crew to other mountains.

  • Under this year's estimate the staff pension is about $60M short. |||
  • Staff statements still describe the pension as secure. ||
  • The board has been told the gap could widen if markets stay down. ||
  • Skipping a payment this year is legal. |

Each director's private conflict

Penalty: You are disbarred if the board is caught hiding a heavy fact.

Goal B: Your firm bills Summit $2M a year. One caught fact ends it, so none may be caught.

Goal B: You were granted stock options last year. The stock must end above where it started.

Penalty: Your investors pull their money if the stock ends below where it started.

Goal A: Your loan terms hold only if the lifts run. Chair 7 must stay open.

Goal B: Your fund quietly owns a rival ski company. The stock must finish in the bottom half.

Lumen Search

Search and AI platform

The scenario

A search engine and AI assistant with two billion queries a day, paid for by an ad business. Co-founder Idris Valdane still holds 25% of the vote.

The real case behind it

Round 1: Facebook video metrics (2016 to 2019). Facebook said in 2016 it overstated average video watch time, and paid $40M in 2019 to settle advertiser claims.

Round 2: OpenAI board (2023). OpenAI's board removed Sam Altman, saying he was not consistently candid. 745 of 770 staff threatened to leave and he returned.

Fix to ask for: Correct a known error fast, and be able to defend your reasons for a firing.

The dilemma, round by round

Round 1. Lumen's ad dashboard reports average view time, but a new analysis shows it leaves out views under three seconds. The analysis is not final, and the cutoff sits in a footnote of the technical guide.

Motion: Credit advertisers for the disputed views and fix the dashboard

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -2 (Shareholders), Customers +2 (Advertisers), Public +1 (The public)

The case for silence: The analysis is unfinished, the cutoff is documented, and nervous advertisers could pull spending the numbers may not justify.

  • The dashboard's average view time leaves out views under three seconds, which raises the average. |||
  • The gap could be as large as 60 to 80 percent, or much smaller. The analysis is not final. ||
  • Some advertisers bought larger campaigns after seeing those numbers. ||
  • The cutoff appears only in a footnote of the technical guide. |

Round 2. Several directors say co-founder Idris Valdane has not been fully candid with them. He says the board had what it needed. Most engineers say they would follow him, and no reason has been given to him in writing.

Motion: Remove co-founder Idris Valdane as CEO

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -1 (Shareholders), Workers -2 (Engineers and staff)

The case for silence: Saying why could start a legal fight and spook lenders, and it would be unfair to Idris if the concerns turn out to be small.

  • Several directors say Idris has not been fully candid with the board. |||
  • Idris met outside investors about a side project without telling the board, and he calls that routine. ||
  • Most engineers have said they would follow Idris if he left. ||
  • No reason has been given to Idris in writing. |

Thread & Co.

Apparel brand

The scenario

A mid-priced apparel brand with 900 stores. Everything is made at 40 supplier factories in Karvan and two other countries, and the largest Karvan factory employs 2,000 people. Founder Elodie Marchetti holds 26% of the vote.

The real case behind it

Round 1: H&M Conscious (2019 to 2022). Norway's consumer authority said in 2019 that H&M's Conscious marketing lacked the information to back its claims.

Round 2: Rana Plaza (2013). Rana Plaza collapsed in Bangladesh on 24 April 2013, killing 1,134 garment workers a day after cracks appeared.

Fix to ask for: Audit the building, not only the wages, and back every green claim.

The dilemma, round by round

Round 1. Thread's Green Line is labeled 'made with recycled materials.' Tests on three garments found between 5% and 60% recycled fiber. A supplier changed the fiber mix two seasons ago to meet a cost target, and the label was not updated.

Motion: Drop the 'recycled' claim from the Green Line label

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -1 (Shareholders), Customers -1 (Shoppers), Public +2 (The public)

The case for silence: 'Made with recycled materials' is true of some garments, three samples prove little, and a retraction could hurt a line shoppers like.

  • Tests on three garments found between 5% and 60% recycled fiber, and the label does not say how much. |||
  • A supplier changed the fiber mix two seasons ago to meet a cost target. ||
  • The label was not updated after the change. ||
  • No lab has tested the whole line. |

Round 2. An inspector's note reports cracks in a wall of the largest Karvan factory and asks for a structural check. The factory says it cannot pay for one while Thread's $18M in invoices is unpaid. Spring orders are due in six weeks.

Motion: Pay the Karvan factory $18M now, tied to a structural check and repairs

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -2 (Shareholders), Workers +2 (Supplier factory workers), Community +2 (Karvan (supplier country)), Public +1 (The public)

The case for silence: The cracks may be cosmetic, a check takes weeks, and a public warning could stall an order that 2,000 people depend on.

  • An inspector's note reports cracks in a wall of the largest Karvan factory and asks for a structural check. |||
  • The factory says it cannot pay for repairs while Thread owes it $18M. ||
  • Thread's audits check wages and hours but not the building. ||
  • Spring orders are due in six weeks. |

Heartland Beef

Beef-on-dairy packer

The scenario

One big packing plant in Prairie Bend, 1,400 line workers, and about 600 ranches that sell it cattle. Founder Walt Dreyer started as a rancher and holds 28% of the vote.

The real case behind it

Round 1: Wells Fargo (2013 to 2020). Wells Fargo paid $185M in 2016 over unauthorized accounts. Its board heard of the risk in 2014 but got incomplete numbers.

Round 2: Theranos (2015 to 2022). Theranos raised over $700M. Its founder was sentenced in 2022 to 11 years 3 months. Critics noted a board of famous names with little medical expertise.

Fix to ask for: Ask for the raw numbers, and seat directors who can read them.

The dilemma, round by round

Round 1. To hit quotas, some Heartland cattle buyers enrolled ranchers in a paid Premium Program. The hotline logged 212 complaints last year. HR reviewed some and calls most of them misunderstandings.

Motion: End buyer sign-up quotas and refund Premium Program fees

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners -2 (Shareholders), Community +2 (Ranchers' co-op), Public +1 (The public)

The case for silence: Most complaints may be misunderstandings, ranchers did sign something, and ending the program would blow a hole in this year's numbers.

  • The hotline logged 212 complaints from ranchers about Premium Program fees they did not expect. |||
  • Buyers get bonuses for sign-ups and lose their jobs for missing quota. ||
  • HR reviewed some complaints and called most of them misunderstandings. ||
  • Board reports mentioned sign-up growth but not the complaints. |

Round 2. Founder Walt Dreyer wants $60M for TraceMark, a cattle-tracing system built by his nephew's startup. It has run only in demos. Nobody on the board has run a tracing system.

Motion: Put $60M into the founder's TraceMark system and announce it to buyers

Who gains or pays if it passes: Owners +2 (Shareholders), Workers -1 (Line workers), Customers +1 (Grocers and diners)

The case for silence: Demos are how every new system starts, the engineers may have left for ordinary reasons, and public doubt could kill a bet that may pay.

  • No one on the board has run a tracing system or read its test data. |||
  • TraceMark has only been shown in demos run by Walt's nephew's team. ||
  • Two TraceMark engineers left last spring, and the startup calls it ordinary turnover. ||
  • No outside lab has tested it. |
