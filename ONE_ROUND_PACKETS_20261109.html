What Jensen's complexity consists of

  • A fictional pharma firm with a long briefing of about 970 words: a short setting paragraph and 11 fact bullets, plus a one-page set of three options and a sample agenda.
  • Three numbered options on one decision, none clearly right: recall the drug, add a warning label and stop promotion, or keep marketing and fight the regulator.
  • Fourteen role cards (6 directors, 8 officers), 41 to 111 words each (mean 69). Each card gives a role, committees, a stake (shares, options, consulting contracts, a mansion built for the CEO) and a conflicting pull. A few cards are thin on purpose (Stone, Garcia, Vance).
  • Ambiguity: the independent study was "not large enough to be conclusive"; no one knows if the drug caused deaths. Fines range from $50 million to $5 billion. A trial costs $12 million and three years.
  • Facts that pull both ways: patient testimonials versus "KILLER" comments; doctors leaving for a rival against a better drug; one-third of revenue and half of profit at risk; a label cuts sales in half; the CIO holds unreleased trial files; a rival drug arrives next month; an IPO needs a stable price.
  • Debrief in four sections (reactions, governance, recommendations, ethics), with agency theory in section 2. The real Vioxx case is told as the reveal. Time to talk, with a recommended 20 minutes of debrief.

Where the packets differ from Jensen, on purpose

  • Role count: Jensen has 14 cards. The game has five director seats per board and no outside role now, so each packet has 5 cards, one per seat, at Jensen's length. Add attending-officer cards (CFO, head of research, PR) if you want 8 more. I held at five because the game cannot seat more.
  • Briefing length: each packet now runs about 960 to 975 words with 11 to 15 fact bullets, against Jensen's 967, and role cards average about 70 words. The extra length comes from facts that pull in opposite directions, ranges, unconfirmed evidence, and a competitor or deadline.
  • Options: four per board where Jensen has three, as you allowed.

Valley Health: Special Board Meeting to Resolve the Billing Audit

Real case behind it: Columbia/HCA, 1997. Briefing 958 words, 12 fact bullets, 4 options, 5 role cards averaging 74 words.

Briefing

It is March 1997 and a special meeting of the Valley Health board has been called. Valley is a regional system of two hospitals, nine clinics and 6,000 nurses and techs, built by Dr. Evelyn Marsh from the Marrow Creek rural clinic. The board is small, so every director sits on several committees, and directors are paid partly in Valley stock that can be sold only in set windows. Valley is publicly traded and its stock has tripled in four years, mostly because it buys hospitals and then raises the profit on each one. The meeting room is above the Marrow Creek clinic, and the board has until the end of the day to decide what Valley will say to the government.

  • Last week federal agents served search warrants at two Valley hospitals and at the offices of several physicians who have business ties to the system. The warrants name Medicare billing, no charges have been filed, and the agents did not say what they were looking for. Outside counsel expects a request for documents within days and says Valley can choose to cooperate before it is asked, though counsel cannot say whether that choice would be rewarded.
  • Medicare pays hospitals a fixed amount for each diagnosis group, and a sicker diagnosis pays more. Valley's internal audit team, which reports to the Chief Financial Officer, finished a review of pneumonia admissions in January and found that over three years the share of cases coded in the higher-paying group rose from 11% to 34% while the patients did not get sicker. Two earlier reviews by Medicare's own contractor, each based on about thirty charts, found no problem with Valley's coding.
  • The coding guidance that produced the rise came from a consulting firm hired by Dr. Marsh's office. The firm called the guidance "aggressive but defensible" and wrote that Medicare rules on the point are unclear, and its fee was a share of the added revenue. Valley's billing staff say they were told that coders were rated on how many cases they moved to the higher group, though no one has produced the rating sheets.
  • The extra Medicare payments from the pattern are estimated at $40 million to $110 million over three years. The range is wide because nobody has reviewed individual charts, and some of the higher codes were probably correct. A chart review of a fair sample would take about four months and cost $3 million, and the government may not wait that long before it names its own number.
  • Valley files two cost reports with Medicare each year. The audit team found that the version kept in the finance office includes a set of costs that the filed version leaves out. The finance office says the second set is a planning document, counsel says there are legitimate reasons to keep one, and counsel also says the gap is the first thing an agent will ask about.
  • Nurses at one hospital sent a letter to the Chief Nursing Officer last year saying they were pressured to document patients as sicker than they were. The letter was filed and never taken to the board. The nurses' association says some of its members would speak to investigators if asked, and a reporter from the regional paper called the Chief Nursing Officer on Monday to ask about the letter.
  • Valley earns about 9% of its revenue from Medicare billing that relies on the disputed codes, and the system earns about $180 million a year in profit. Paying back $110 million would cut a year of earnings by more than half. The bonds carry a covenant that a large unexpected charge could trigger, and the lenders are scheduled to meet in three weeks.
  • Other large hospital chains have been fined for billing errors in the past, and the amounts ran from a few million dollars to several hundred million. Settlements were larger where regulators found that executives knew about a pattern and kept it going. Regulators have also been more lenient with firms that came forward before an investigation began, although Valley's warrants were served before anyone at Valley came forward.
  • Dr. Marsh holds 6% of the stock and has told directors she knew nothing about the coding guidance. She has offered to step aside if the board asks, but she wants to stay until the bond sale is done, and she has asked the board to close the weakest rural clinics. Doctors in those towns say the clinics, which serve mostly Medicare patients, will not survive a large repayment.
  • Two physician partners have hired their own lawyers, and one has hinted to Valley's counsel that he would cooperate with the agents if he were offered protection. He says he knows of payments tied to referrals, but nobody has confirmed it, and the physicians' group says it will settle its own position by Friday.
  • Valley's auditors have said the year-end statements will need a note about the investigation however the board decides, and that the wording will differ depending on whether Valley has acknowledged a problem. The directors' liability insurer pays legal fees but excludes known fraud, and it has asked to be told what the board knew and when.
  • The stock fell 14% on the news of the warrants. It stands at $31, down from $36 a week ago, with about 40 million shares outstanding. The board is also weighing a bond sale to buy a third hospital, and the seller's option expires on April 30 with a competing bidder waiting. A rival system in the next county announced that it had repaid Medicare after finding its own coding errors, called the repayment a routine correction, and saw its stock barely move, although it came forward before any warrants were served.

The decision

Valley's senior management team has been asked to attend the meeting. The Board Chair, who is also the CEO, plans to open by proposing four alternatives. The board must select one by the end of the meeting:

  1. Disclose and repay. Report the coding pattern to Medicare now, offer to repay the difference once a sample chart review is done, and hire outside counsel and an outside reviewer who report to the board.
  2. Fix quietly. Change the coding guidance and the coder ratings immediately, keep the second cost report and the past payments in place, and tell the government only what it asks for.
  3. Defend the coding. Keep the guidance, treat the pattern as an honest reading of unclear rules, retain the consulting firm and a litigation team, and cooperate on the narrowest terms the law allows.
  4. Reset the leadership. Place Dr. Marsh and the Chief Financial Officer on leave, put an interim leader in charge, and let that leader decide what to disclose after a sixty-day internal review.

Reveal (read only in the debrief)

The real firm was Columbia/HCA, then the largest hospital chain in the United States. In March 1997 federal agents served search warrants in El Paso, Texas, and the investigation widened across the country. CEO Rick Scott resigned in July 1997. What the board did and did not know in those months is not documented well enough to say here, and the company did not come forward before the investigation. Subsidiaries pleaded guilty to fraud charges in December 2000, in a settlement of about $840 million that included upcoding of pneumonia diagnoses. A 2003 civil settlement of $631 million, plus a $250 million cost-report payment, brought recoveries to about $1.7 billion.

Debrief questions

  1. Agency theory says the founder, the managers and the owners want different things. Which of the five directors had an interest that differed from the owners' interest, and what did each do about it in your vote?
  2. The consulting firm called the coding aggressive but defensible. How does information asymmetry between billing staff, the audit team and the board make a quiet fix attractive, and who bore the risk of that choice?
  3. The nurses' letter was filed and never reached the board. What governance mechanism should have carried it up, and which agent was supposed to run it?
  4. If the rival system's repayment had not been in the briefing, would your board have chosen differently? What does it tell you about how boards copy peers under uncertainty?

Summit Peaks: Special Board Meeting to Resolve the Series 7 Lift Crisis

Real case behind it: Boeing 737 MAX, 2019. Briefing 972 words, 13 fact bullets, 4 options, 5 role cards averaging 66 words.

Briefing

It is March 2019 and a special meeting of the Summit Peaks board has been called. Summit runs six mountains on one season pass, built by Dana Strand from a single hill in Alder Creek. The board is small, so each director sits on several committees, and the company has no board committee that looks at safety. Directors are paid in company stock, and Summit is publicly traded. The company earns most of its money from the Series 7 gondola, a lift it designs and sells to other resorts as well as running on its own mountains, and the board has been told that regulators and the press will wait for its decision.

  • Two Series 7 gondola cabins have fallen from their cables in five months. The first fall, in October at a partner resort, killed 18 riders. The second, last Sunday at Summit's own Alder Creek mountain, killed 12 riders and one lift operator. Government safety inspectors have closed lifts of the same model at other resorts but have not yet ordered Summit's remaining 41 Series 7 lifts to stop, and their preliminary report points to the software while allowing that a worn grip may have played a part.
  • After the first fall Summit told its customers that the Series 7 was safe and that the fall was caused by crew error at the partner resort. A software update to the cable grip was promised within six weeks and has been delayed three times. Summit's engineers say the update could be ready in about eleven weeks, but regulators will need to test it, and the earliest approval anyone expects is August.
  • The cable grip software reads a single sensor. Summit chose a single sensor to speed up the design, and it sold a second sensor as an optional extra. Operator manuals mention the software in one line and do not say that a wrong sensor reading can push the cabin off the cable. Summit's engineers say operators who knew would have trained differently, and others say no training would have saved the cabin.
  • A senior engineer sent an internal message two years ago saying the design had not been tested the way a lift that carries people should be. It went to a supervisor, who answered that schedule came first. The message sits in a file and the board has not seen it. A survey of 300 Summit engineers last year found that 40% did not feel free to raise safety concerns, though the survey was anonymous and fewer than half responded.
  • The Series 7, Summit's best-selling product, brings in $2.1 billion of the company's $9 billion in revenue and has a backlog of 4,000 orders. Another 2,000 Series 7 units are in construction and cannot be shipped unless inspectors permit it. Two resort chains have already cancelled orders for 90 lifts, and several others say they will wait for the regulators before deciding.
  • If Summit stops building, it must pay suppliers about $1 billion over the next year and lay off thousands, and skilled lift workers who leave might not return. If it keeps building and the inspectors later find another defect, the company could face criminal and civil claims and could lose its operating license, and nobody inside the company can say which risk is larger.
  • The company has been spending about $4 billion a year on dividends and share buybacks, and the board approved a new $6 billion buyback authorization in January. Its stock has fallen 12% since the second fall and stands at $380, with 570 million shares outstanding. The fund partners on the board want the buybacks to continue because they signal strength.
  • The Chair, who is also the CEO, is respected, has spent thirty years in the lift business, and believes the lift is sound and the software fix will prove it. He has asked the board for time and has said that a recall would send the wrong signal. The audit committee chair adds that the company's internal safety group reports to the CEO's office and not to the board.
  • A competitor with an older lift design carries the same rider numbers. It has offered to take Summit's resort customers if Summit leaves them without service, and some resorts have already asked about switching. Summit's sales team thinks most customers would come back after a fix, and the finance team is less sure.
  • Regulators in two other countries closed Series 7 lifts last week. The home regulator has taken weeks longer, and Summit's lobbyists say a closure is likely within days. Regulators who find a company slow to act can impose penalties far larger than those for a company that acts first, but a company that grounds its own lifts also admits that they are unsafe.
  • Families of the 18 riders killed in October have hired lawyers, and insurers have said that coverage will depend on whether Summit knew of the defect before the second fall. A congressional committee has asked for company documents by April 15. The board has not seen a safety report since the first fall, and directors say the CEO told them each time that engineering was handling it.
  • Summit's insurance broker says the company carries $2 billion of liability coverage, which would cover the families' claims at the lower end of the estimates and not at the upper end. The broker has also warned that a decision to keep flying after a known software defect could void part of the coverage, while a decision to ground the lifts could be taken as an admission in court.
  • Skiers are still buying season passes for next winter, and early sales are down 9% from last year. Summit's marketing head says a visible safety review would win some of them back, and the Chair says any review will make the numbers worse.

The decision

Summit's senior management team has been asked to attend the meeting. The Board Chair, who is also the CEO, plans to open by proposing four alternatives. The board must select one by the end of the meeting:

  1. Stop everything. Close every Series 7 lift now, halt production and shipments, hire an outside safety review that reports straight to the board, and withhold dividends and buybacks until it finishes.
  2. Fix and keep running. Close only Alder Creek and the lifts that regulators order closed, keep building lifts while the software fix is finished, and release a public statement that the fix will make the Series 7 safe.
  3. Keep building and shield the company. Keep every Series 7 lift open, continue production, rely on inspectors and lobbyists to set the timetable, and defend the company's record in public.
  4. Change the leadership. Separate the Chair and CEO roles at once, form a board safety committee with the power to see all engineering messages, and leave the operating decision to the new committee after a three-week review.

Debrief questions

  4. Buybacks and dividends were running while the safety question was open. How does a payout to owners signal confidence, and what did your board's vote say about whose interest came first?

Lumen Search: Special Board Meeting to Decide on the CEO

Real case behind it: OpenAI board, November 2023. Briefing 943 words, 13 fact bullets, 4 options, 5 role cards averaging 70 words.

Briefing

It is a Friday in November and a special meeting of the Lumen Search board has been called, with no notice to the CEO. Lumen runs a search engine and an AI assistant with two billion queries a day. It began as a research lab started by Idris Valdane, with a charter that says the lab exists to build AI that is safe and widely shared. A for-profit arm was added later to pay for computing, and the charter gives the lab's board, not the investors, the final word. The board has six seats, three held by people who work at Lumen and three who do not. Lumen is privately held, and a large technology company has put in billions and runs Lumen's computing. The company's developer conference opens on Monday with 2,000 outside guests.

  • The CEO, Sam Hartigan, is the face of the company. He has raised the money that lets the lab buy its computing, he recruited most of the senior staff himself, and the product has the fastest user growth in the industry. The board hired him five years ago, and he sits on the board. Several staff members told directors he is generous with credit and slow to share bad news.
  • Three directors, including the chief scientist, believe the CEO has not been fully honest with the board, citing a product launch the board learned of from the press, a safety review described as finished when it was not, and his sounding out a chip company about a new venture without telling the board. Two of the three events are documented in emails, and the third rests on one director's notes.
  • The CEO says each of those events was a misunderstanding. He says he told the board what he thought it needed, and that a company moving this fast cannot send every decision to a six-person committee. Several former board members agree that earlier boards were kept lightly informed, and they say it was the arrangement the board accepted when it hired him.
  • The charter says the board's duty is to the mission and not to profit. It also says that if the lab builds powerful AI that could be unsafe, the board must be able to slow or stop its release. No one has told the board that such a system exists yet, though the chief scientist has hinted at one, and he has not shown the other directors anything to back the hint.
  • Nearly all of the 770 employees hold equity that depends on a coming share sale at an $86 billion valuation. A tender offer is set for December 15, and a delay past year-end would let the buyer withdraw. If the CEO leaves, the sale might fail. Many staff have said privately that they follow the CEO, not the board, though some also say they are uneasy about the pace of releases.
  • The technology partner owns a large share of the for-profit arm and provides the computing, and it was told nothing in advance. It has said it expects a good explanation and that its contract would let it hire the CEO's team if the lab falls apart. Its own chief executive has said in public that he is committed to Lumen, and he told one director by phone last night that he is frustrated.
  • The board has no written findings and has hired no law firm, and no investigator has talked to the staff. The directors who want action have spent months raising concerns one at a time, and each time the CEO has offered an answer. The bylaws let a majority of the outside directors remove the CEO without cause, but counsel warns that the first lawsuit would ask what the cause was.
  • Two of the three directors who do not work at Lumen are new to the board. One has written papers on AI policy that criticize Lumen's release speed. The other is a technology founder who owns a competing product. Each could be accused of acting out of self-interest, and the CEO's allies have already said so privately.
  • The chief scientist holds a concern the others do not share, that the CEO is pushing too fast toward a system the lab cannot control. He also likes the CEO and worries that firing him would break the company he built. Last month 40 engineers circulated a letter asking for slower releases, and 12 of them signed, but none has said so to the full board.
  • The company's president, the CEO's closest ally and a board member, will quit if the CEO is removed. The chief technology officer has agreed to serve as interim CEO but cannot say how long she can hold the company together. A rival lab has told several of Lumen's best researchers that it will match their equity, and one has already accepted.
  • Reporters have asked for comment on a rumor that the board is meeting, and a leak is expected within hours. The board has no account of the lab without the CEO or of the lab after backing down, and either story would reach the staff before Monday.
  • The board's last two meetings ran under an hour each and had no written agenda. Two of the outside directors have asked for the minutes, and the CEO's office says the minutes are being edited. Each side reads the delay as evidence for its own view.
  • A competitor's model, released last week, matches Lumen's flagship on most public tests, and the CEO has argued that a leadership crisis now would hand the lead away. The chief scientist replies that the lead is the reason no one will slow down.

The decision

The board's chair plans to open by presenting four alternatives. The board must select one by the end of the meeting:

  1. Remove the CEO now. Announce his departure today, name the chief technology officer interim CEO, and say in public that he was not consistently honest with the board.
  2. Put him on notice. Give the CEO a written list of what the board needs from him, set a ninety-day review, keep his seat, and tell the staff and the partner what was agreed.
  3. Investigate first. Hire an outside law firm to interview staff and report in thirty days, ask the CEO to step back from board decisions meanwhile, and say nothing in public.
  4. Expand the board. Add three independent directors with business experience, move the CEO off the board, and let the larger board decide his future in the spring.

Thread & Co.: Special Board Meeting to Resolve the Factory Safety Agreement

Real case behind it: Rana Plaza and the Bangladesh Accord, 2012-2013. Briefing 959 words, 15 fact bullets, 4 options, 5 role cards averaging 64 words.

Briefing

It is January 2013 and a special meeting of the Thread & Co. board has been called. Thread is a mid-priced apparel brand with 900 stores, started by Elodie Marchetti and her first design partner. Everything it sells is made at 40 supplier factories in Karvan and two other countries, none of them owned by Thread, and the largest Karvan factory employs 2,000 people. The board is small, so each director sits on several committees, and directors are paid partly in company stock. Thread is publicly traded, and most of its profit comes from a low cost per garment that Karvan makes possible. A coalition of labor groups has asked every large buyer for an answer by 15 February.

  • In November a fire at a Karvan garment factory killed 112 workers, who were trapped behind locked and blocked exits. Thread did not buy from that factory, but two of its competitors did, and photographs of burned labels have appeared in the press. A reporter from a national paper has asked Thread to list its Karvan suppliers by Friday and has not said what the paper will do with the list.
  • Since then a coalition of labor groups and a few European brands has circulated a proposed binding safety agreement. Brands that sign would pay for independent inspections of every factory they use, publish the reports, share the cost of repairs, and stop ordering from any factory that refuses to fix serious hazards. The agreement runs five years and workers' unions could enforce it in court. The coalition says the legal text is still being drafted and the terms may change.
  • Only two companies, both European, have signed. The coalition says it will announce the agreement once enough large buyers join, and it hears that a third brand will sign next month if one more large buyer does. Several large buyers in North America have said they will not accept binding terms that expose them to lawsuits, and have offered their own voluntary safety programs instead.
  • Thread already pays for social audits at all 40 suppliers. The audits are done by a private firm on an announced schedule and check wages, hours and exits. They do not examine the structure of the building, and the firm has said that structural checks would need a different contract. Locked exits were the cause of the November deaths, and Thread's audits would have been expected to catch them.
  • Thread's own sourcing staff estimate that a proper structural inspection of its Karvan factories would find that between 10% and 30% of them need major repairs. They cannot say more because no engineer has been inside most of them. Many of the buildings are older than the garment trade and some have floors added on top.
  • The owner of Thread's largest Karvan factory is a cousin of a government minister. He has told Thread's sourcing head that an inspection will find nothing and that he will move to a rival buyer if Thread forces repairs. Sourcing believes the factory has added a floor, though no one at Thread has seen the plans.
  • The binding agreement is projected to cost Thread about $12 million over five years in inspections and repair contributions, against annual earnings of $210 million. Karvan factories have said that if buyers force repairs, some owners will close rather than pay, and about 3,000 workers per closed factory could lose their jobs.
  • Karvan is a poor country, and garments are 80% of its exports. The government has said it welcomes better safety but warns that buyers who walk away would hurt the workers they say they want to protect, and it will not pay for repairs. The trade association says other countries are waiting to take Karvan's orders.
  • Customers in Thread's home market have started to ask where their clothes are made. A survey Thread commissioned says 60% of customers say they care and 9% say they would pay more. Thread's largest department-store customer has asked in writing whether Thread's factories are inspected, and Thread's mid-priced rivals are cutting prices.
  • A voluntary program offered by other buyers would cost Thread about $3 million a year, run by the buyers themselves, with no union role and no legal duty to pay for repairs. Labor groups call it a way to appear active without taking on liability, and its first public report would not appear for a year. Thread's closest competitor has said it will wait to see what the trade association proposes.
  • Thread's stock is at $22, with 90 million shares outstanding, and the stock moves little on safety news. Investors have not yet asked about Karvan, though one pension fund that holds 3% has sent a letter asking how Thread monitors building safety.
  • Thread's legal staff say that a binding commitment creates a duty that a voluntary program does not. They also say that a voluntary program does not protect a company that knew of hazards and did not act, and that a court would treat an unread inspection report as knowledge.
  • Thread's insurer has said that its liability policy covers losses abroad only if the company followed reasonable safety practice, and no one can say what a court would call reasonable. The same insurer has offered a small premium discount to companies that commission structural inspections.
  • Thread's two most senior buyers disagree, one calling the largest Karvan factories the safest and the other the most overbuilt and most likely to fail, and neither has an engineer's report to cite, and the report would take three months and about $400,000 to commission.
  • A well-known actress has agreed to front Thread's spring campaign, and her representatives have asked whether the company will sign. The contract has a morals clause that could cut either way.

The decision

Thread's senior management team has been asked to attend the meeting. The Board Chair, who is also the CEO, plans to open by proposing four alternatives. The board must select one by the end of the meeting:

  1. Sign the binding agreement. Commit to independent inspections, public reports, shared repair costs and a rule against ordering from factories that refuse to fix serious hazards.
  2. Join the buyers' voluntary program. Pay into the program run by other brands, commit to inspections without a legal duty to fund repairs, and publish a summary report each year.
  3. Strengthen our own audits. Add structural inspections to Thread's existing audit contract, share the results with suppliers privately, and decide on repairs factory by factory.
  4. Reduce exposure. Move orders away from the riskiest Karvan factories to other countries over twelve months, and sign nothing until the new supplier base is in place.

Reveal (read only in the debrief)

The real case is the Bangladesh garment industry. A fire at the Tazreen Fashions factory on 24 November 2012 killed 112 workers. PVH and Tchibo had already signed earlier agreements with labor groups, and the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was drafted in the months that followed, with the text dated 13 May 2013. Debrief questions

  2. Thread, as principal, could push risk onto a supplier it does not own. Which option moved risk back onto the brand, and why would owners resist that?
  3. The workers were the people with the most to lose and the least say. What would a board have to do to give them a real voice, and which of your directors was the closest agent for them?
  4. A voluntary program lets a company appear active without taking on a legal duty. When does appearing responsible differ from being responsible, and how can an owner tell?

Heartland Beef: Special Board Meeting to Resolve the Program Enrollment Problem

Real case behind it: Wells Fargo sales practices, 2013-2016. Briefing 966 words, 14 fact bullets, 4 options, 5 role cards averaging 71 words.

Briefing

It is February 2014 and a special meeting of the Heartland Beef board has been called. Heartland runs one big packing plant in Prairie Bend, with 1,400 line workers and about 600 ranches that sell it cattle. Walt Dreyer, the founder, started as a rancher and holds 28% of the vote. The board is small, so each director sits on several committees, and directors are paid partly in company stock. Heartland is publicly traded, and its growth story is that it sells more products to each customer than any other packer. The annual shareholder meeting is in five weeks.

  • For six years Heartland has paid its field buyers and sales reps a bonus based on how many of its programs each rancher or grocery account joins. The programs include a branded-beef contract, a weighing service with a monthly fee, a financing line, and a feed-supply plan. The internal slogan is "five programs per account," and it hangs on the wall of every regional office.
  • The company's average is now 4.6 programs per account, up from 2.1. Wall Street analysts cite the number as proof that Heartland's customers are loyal, and the company's lenders watch it as well. Walt Dreyer has said in public that the number shows the company's culture, and he repeated it to employees last month.
  • A newspaper has asked about reps who enrolled ranchers in the weighing service and the financing line without their knowledge. Some ranchers found monthly fees deducted from their cattle payments and did not know why. The newspaper says it has talked to former employees in three states, plans to publish in ten days, and has asked Heartland for comment by Friday.
  • Heartland's own ethics hotline logged 650 complaints about enrollment in the last two years. About 200 of them came from reps who said they were told to hit the numbers by any means. The hotline reports to the head of the sales division, who has called most of the complaints isolated. The hotline vendor says the count is higher than at similar firms, but it cannot say whether that signals a problem or a hotline that people trust.
  • Last year the company fired 230 field staff for enrolling accounts without consent, mostly at the lowest levels. Several of those fired say a manager told them to enroll accounts, and Heartland's lawyers have not interviewed them. Human resources estimates that the true number of improper enrollments is at least 10 times the number caught, but there is no sample review to back the estimate.
  • The division head, Karen Mueller, is the most successful executive in the company. Her division brings in 60% of the profit. She says the enrollment problem is a handful of employees who cheated, and that the targets themselves are fair and tough. Her bonus depends on the program count, and she has told directors she will not run a division she cannot hold to a number.
  • If the targets are scrapped, analysts will read it as an admission. Sales staff might sell less, and the program count would drop, probably by half in two years. The stock could fall by 15% or more, and it has a high price based on the count. A rival packer in the next state dropped its own program-count bonus last year, lost two of its best reps, and reports that customer complaints fell by a third.
  • If the targets stay and the newspaper is right, the company could face fines, ranchers' lawsuits and regulators. In similar cases elsewhere, fines have run from tens of millions of dollars to several hundred million, and executives have lost their jobs. Regulators have punished firms that knew of a pattern and did not change the incentives, and they have been gentler with firms that changed course before a story ran.
  • Heartland's total improper fees are estimated at $4 million to $40 million. The wide range exists because no one has checked the bank records. Refunding the fees with an apology would cost the low end quickly and the high end over a year, and the refund itself could be read as an admission.
  • Ranchers in the co-op are angry and talking about selling their cattle to a rival packer. About 600 ranches supply the plant, and a boycott of even a quarter of them would leave the plant short of cattle. For many of them Heartland is the only buyer within a day's haul, so a boycott would also cost the ranchers.
  • Heartland's stock is at $52, with 120 million shares outstanding. The board has been told about the sales practices before, in short slides, as a low-level risk. It has never asked to see the complaint data, and the audit committee chair says she cannot recall the hotline numbers ever being shown to her.
  • Two of the five directors sit on the audit committee and say they asked about the hotline in 2012 and were told the numbers were normal for a company of Heartland's size. Neither can find a record of the question, and management says it has no record of the answer.
  • Heartland's chief financial officer says that if the board refunds fees and drops the bonus, the company will report a one-time charge and a weak quarter, and the bank that holds the plant's credit line has the right to reprice it if earnings miss two quarters in a row. The CFO adds that the charge now would likely cost less than a year of continuing losses.
  • A state agriculture regulator called the Chair on Tuesday to ask for a copy of the standard rancher contract, and did not say why. A former sales rep has also posted a long account online, which has been shared about 4,000 times and has not been checked by anyone at Heartland.

The decision

Heartland's senior management team has been asked to attend the meeting. The Board Chair, who is also the CEO, plans to open by proposing four alternatives. The board must select one by the end of the meeting:

  1. End the targets. Drop the program-count bonus for all staff now, refund every disputed fee, hire an outside firm to review every enrollment, and move the ethics hotline to report to the board.
  2. Keep the targets and police them. Keep the bonus plan, add enrollment confirmation calls to ranchers, fire anyone caught, and have human resources report quarterly on terminations.
  3. Review before deciding. Keep the plan for now, hire an outside firm to study the incentive plan and report in six months, and tell the newspaper the company takes the matter seriously.
  4. Change the people. Move Karen Mueller out of the sales division, appoint an interim head, and let that head decide on the targets after a ninety-day review.

Reveal (read only in the debrief)

The real firm was Wells Fargo. For years the bank set cross-selling goals under the slogan "Gr-eight," a push for eight products per household, and employees opened accounts customers had not asked for. In September 2016 regulators fined the bank $185 million (CFPB $100 million, Los Angeles City Attorney $50 million, OCC $35 million), and the SEC filing reports about 5,300 employees terminated between January 2011 and March 2016. The independent directors' 2017 report found that management first flagged sales-practice problems to the board in February 2014 and that later reporting was incomplete and inaccurate, and some directors said a May 2015 presentation had left them believing 200 to 300 people had been fired. The bank dropped its sales goals, and CEO John Stumpf left in October 2016. In April 2017 the board announced about $75 million in new recoveries, including a $28 million clawback from Stumpf, and said forfeitures and adjustments for Stumpf (about $69 million) and Carrie Tolstedt, head of the community bank (about $67 million), were part of a total above $180 million across senior leaders.

Debrief questions

  1. Agency theory says incentives pull an agent toward what is measured. What was measured at Heartland, what was left unmeasured, and what behavior did your board expect from the bonus plan?
  2. The hotline reported to the sales division head. Why does the reporting line of a monitoring device matter, and which option moved it?
  3. Firing the lowest-level staff fixed the individuals and left the incentive in place. Who bore the cost of that choice, and who benefited?
  4. The board had seen the problem on short slides as a low-level risk. What does it take for a board to demand raw data instead of summaries, and what held your directors back?
